The obvious question

What's going on here?

February 14, 2024

Jamie McCourt — former Los Angeles Dodgers CEO and a major Trump fundraiser — donated $10,000 to the Republican Party of Guam.

McCourt's was the first of 193 donations of exactly $10,000 that GOP Guam would receive in 2024.

$10,000 was the maximum amount an individual could contribute to a state party committee for the year.

And every one of those 193 contributions came through the same entity:

Trump 47 Committee Inc.

What is Trump 47?

Trump 47 Committee Inc. was a joint fundraising committee — an entity that allowed several political committees to raise money together.

Think of it as a group tip jar.

A donor could write one enormous check. Trump 47 would then allocate portions of that contribution among Trump's campaign, Save America PAC, the Republican National Committee and participating state and territorial party committees.

According to the allocation formula described in the original reporting, money reached state-party accounts only after the donor had first contributed the permitted amounts to Trump's campaign, Save America PAC and the RNC.

Suspicions confirmed.

Following the money showed that nearly all of the funds received by the Republican Party of Guam subsequently went to the RNC.

Transfers between national, state and territorial party committees are treated differently from an individual's direct contribution to a party committee.

But why not just give the money to the RNC in the first place?

The donors reaching GOP Guam through Trump 47 had already reached the applicable RNC contribution allocation before money flowed to state-party accounts.

That is what made the territorial party significant: the individual donor faced a contribution limit, while party committees could subsequently transfer funds among affiliated committees.

The larger question isn't only where the money started.
It's where the money ended up.
Guam became an unlikely waypoint in a much larger national campaign-finance story.

The bigger question.

Contribution limits are supposed to restrict how much influence one donor can exercise over a political committee.

But this case illustrates the tension between individual contribution limits and the ability of party committees to move money between themselves.

Whatever one's view of the legality or intent of the system, the path from Trump 47 to Guam and then back to the national party shows how complicated modern campaign finance can become.

Following the first donation isn't enough.

You have to follow where the money goes next.
